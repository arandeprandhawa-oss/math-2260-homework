1Mean, median, trimmed means

Devore §1.3, like Exercise 33, p. 34

A real-estate newsletter reported these sale prices for a sample of 10 homes sold last month (in 1,000s of $).

432   710   498   525   389   1,150   465   540   610   481
(a)

Calculate the sample mean and the sample median, in thousands of dollars.

(b)

Suppose the 6th observation had been 1,450 rather than 1,150. How would the mean and median change?

(c)

Calculate a 20% trimmed mean by trimming the two smallest and two largest observations. Round to the nearest hundred dollars.

(d)

Calculate a 15% trimmed mean. Round to the nearest hundred dollars.

Worked solution

(a) The ten values add to 5,800.

\[\bar{x} = \frac{5800}{10} = 580.0\]

Sorted: 389, 432, 465, 481, 498, 525, 540, 610, 710, 1150. With \(n = 10\), the median averages the 5th and 6th values.

\[\tilde{x} = \frac{498 + 525}{2} = 511.5\]

The average sale price in this sample was $580,000. Half the homes sold for less than $511,500 and half sold for more.

(b) 1,150 is already the largest value, so raising it to 1,450 leaves the 5th and 6th sorted values alone. The median stays at 511.5. The sum rises by 300, so the mean rises to \(6100/10 = 610\).

(c) Drop 389, 432 and 710, 1150.

\[\bar{x}_{tr(20)} = \frac{465+481+498+525+540+610}{6} = \frac{3119}{6} \approx 519.83\]

That is $519,800 to the nearest hundred.

(d) A 15% trim would remove 1.5 values from each end, so average the 10% and 20% trimmed means. The 10% trimmed mean drops only 389 and 1150.

\[\bar{x}_{tr(10)} = \frac{5800 - 389 - 1150}{8} = \frac{4261}{8} = 532.625\] \[\bar{x}_{tr(15)} = \frac{532.625 + 519.833}{2} \approx 526.23\]

That is $526,200 to the nearest hundred.

2Choosing a representative value

Devore §1.3, like Exercise 37, p. 35

October rainfall (mm) at a weather station over ten consecutive years.

62   88   71   95   240   79   84   68   91   77
(a)

Calculate the sample mean and median.

(b)

Which would you report as a typical October rainfall for this period? Say why in one sentence before you check.

Worked solution

(a) The sum is 955, so \(\bar{x} = 95.5\) mm. Sorted: 62, 68, 71, 77, 79, 84, 88, 91, 95, 240.

\[\tilde{x} = \frac{79 + 84}{2} = 81.5\]

(b) Report the median. The single 240 mm year pulls the mean up to 95.5, a value that only one of the ten years actually exceeded. The median of 81.5 mm sits in the middle of the other nine years and is not affected by how extreme that one year was.

3Sensitivity of the median

Devore §1.3, like Exercise 38, p. 35

Blood pressures are reported to the nearest 5 mmHg. The actual values for nine individuals are below.

127.6   132.4   118.9   141.2   136.0   122.3   144.8   131.0   139.5
(a)

What is the median of the reported values?

(b)

Suppose the second individual's value is 132.6 rather than 132.4. What is the new median of the reported values?

(c)

What does this say about the median when data are rounded or grouped?

Worked solution

(a) Reported values: 130, 130, 120, 140, 135, 120, 145, 130, 140. Sorted: 120, 120, 130, 130, 130, 135, 140, 140, 145. The 5th value is 130.

(b) 132.6 rounds to 135 instead of 130. Sorted: 120, 120, 130, 130, 135, 135, 140, 140, 145. The median is now 135.

(c) One actual value moved by 0.2 and the median jumped by 5. When data are rounded or grouped, the median can be highly sensitive to a small change in a single observation near the middle.

4Sample proportion

Devore §1.3, like Exercise 41, p. 36

A sample of \(n = 12\) phones was drop-tested. S means no visible damage and F means damage.

S  F  S  S  F  S  S  S  F  S  S  S
(a)

What is the sample proportion of successes?

(b)

Replace each S with 1 and each F with 0, then calculate \(\bar{x}\). How does it compare with \(x/n\)?

(c)

Suppose 28 more phones are tested. How many of them would have to be S's to give \(x/n = 0.85\) for the entire sample of 40?

Worked solution

(a) There are 9 S's.

\[\frac{x}{n} = \frac{9}{12} = 0.75\]

(b) The coded data are nine 1's and three 0's, so the sum is 9 and \(\bar{x} = 9/12 = 0.75\). For 0/1 data, the sample mean is the sample proportion of successes.

(c) A proportion of 0.85 in 40 phones needs \(0.85 \times 40 = 34\) S's. You already have 9, so \(34 - 9 = 25\) of the 28 new phones must be S's.

5Variance by the shortcut formula

Devore §1.4, like Exercise 44, p. 44

Twelve observations on coating thickness (mm).

3.1   2.8   4.0   3.6   2.5   3.9
4.4   3.2   2.9   3.7   4.1   3.3
(a)

Calculate the two sums.

(b)

Use those sums to compute the sample variance and standard deviation, to four decimal places.

Worked solution

(a) \(\sum x_i = 41.5\) and \(\sum x_i^2 = 147.27\).

(b) Use the shortcut formula for \(S_{xx}\).

\[S_{xx} = \sum x_i^2 - \frac{\left(\sum x_i\right)^2}{n} = 147.27 - \frac{(41.5)^2}{12} = 147.27 - 143.5208 = 3.7492\] \[s^2 = \frac{S_{xx}}{n-1} = \frac{3.7492}{11} = 0.3408\] \[s = \sqrt{0.3408} = 0.5838\]

6Outliers and boxplots

Devore §1.4, like Exercise 57, p. 46

A sample of 20 cables was tested for breaking strength, with this partial information.

median       = 152.4
lower fourth = 147.0
upper fourth = 161.0

Three smallest:    98.6   139.2   143.5
Three largest:    166.8   171.9   190.3
(a)

Find the cutoffs for mild and extreme outliers, then classify the data.

(b)

Construct a boxplot that shows outliers. The box runs from 147.0 to 161.0. Where does each whisker end, and which boxplot below shows this sample?

(c)

What does the box say about the middle half of the data?

Worked solution

(a) The fourth spread is \(f_s = 161.0 - 147.0 = 14.0\), so \(1.5 f_s = 21\) and \(3 f_s = 42\).

\[147.0 - 21 = 126 \qquad 161.0 + 21 = 182\] \[147.0 - 42 = 105 \qquad 161.0 + 42 = 203\]

98.6 is below 105, so it is an extreme outlier. 190.3 is above 182 but below 203, so it is a mild outlier. Every other value lies between 126 and 182.

(b) The whiskers stop at the most extreme non-outliers, 139.2 and 171.9. The two outliers are plotted individually. That is boxplot B. Devore draws a mild outlier as a closed circle and an extreme outlier as an open circle, as in the plot below. The answer options follow WebAssign and draw both as dots.

(c) The median is \(152.4 - 147.0 = 5.4\) above the lower fourth and \(161.0 - 152.4 = 8.6\) below the upper fourth. The upper part of the box is longer, so the middle half of the data is positively skewed.

7Sample space and events

Devore §2.1, like Exercise 4, p. 56

Four circuit boards are inspected, and each is classified as passing (P) or defective (D). Write outcomes like PDDP, separated by commas or spaces. For an empty set, type none.

(a)

List the 16 outcomes in \(\mathcal{S}\).

(b)

Which outcomes are in the event that exactly one board passes?

(c)

Which outcomes are in the event that all four boards are of the same type?

(d)

Which outcomes are in the event that at least three boards are defective?

(e)

What are the union and the intersection of the events in (c) and (d)?

(f)

What are the union and the intersection of the events in (b) and (c)?

Worked solution

(a) There are \(2^4 = 16\) outcomes.

PPPP  PPPD  PPDP  PPDD
PDPP  PDPD  PDDP  PDDD
DPPP  DPPD  DPDP  DPDD
DDPP  DDPD  DDDP  DDDD

(b) {PDDD, DPDD, DDPD, DDDP}

(c) {PPPP, DDDD}

(d) {PDDD, DPDD, DDPD, DDDP, DDDD}

(e) Union: {PPPP, DDDD, PDDD, DPDD, DDPD, DDDP}. Intersection: {DDDD}, the only outcome in both lists.

(f) Union: {PDDD, DPDD, DDPD, DDDP, PPPP, DDDD}. Intersection: \(\varnothing\). No outcome has exactly one pass and also has all four boards alike, so the two events are mutually exclusive.

8Sequential sampling

Devore §2.1, like Exercise 6, p. 57

A drawer holds four batteries. Batteries 1 and 2 are dead, and batteries 3 and 4 are good. A technician tests them in random order and stops as soon as a good battery is found. One possible outcome is 4, and another is 213. Separate outcomes with commas or spaces.

(a)

List the outcomes in \(\mathcal{S}\).

(b)

Let \(A\) be the event that exactly one battery is tested. What outcomes are in \(A\)?

(c)

Let \(B\) be the event that battery 4 is the good one found. What outcomes are in \(B\)?

(d)

Let \(C\) be the event that battery 2 is not tested. What outcomes are in \(C\)?

Worked solution

(a) Testing stops at the first good battery, so each outcome is zero, one, or two dead batteries followed by one good one.

One tested:    3    4
Two tested:    13   14   23   24
Three tested:  123  124  213  214

\(\mathcal{S}\) has 10 outcomes.

(b) \(A = \{3,\ 4\}\)

(c) \(B = \{4,\ 14,\ 24,\ 124,\ 214\}\)

(d) \(C = \{3,\ 4,\ 13,\ 14\}\)

9Events in set notation

Devore §2.1, like Exercise 8, p. 57

A data centre has three servers. Let \(A_i\) be the event that server \(i\) is online at noon. For each event, choose the expression that describes it in terms of \(A_1\), \(A_2\), and \(A_3\), then choose the Venn diagram with that event shaded.

In each diagram the rectangle is the whole sample space, so shading outside the circles means no server is online.

(a)

At least one server is online.

(b)

No server is online.

(c)

Only server 2 is online.

(d)

Exactly two servers are online.

(e)

Either server 3 is online, or both of the other servers are offline.

Worked solution

(a) Everything inside any circle.

\[A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3\]

(b) Only the region outside all three circles.

\[A_1' \cap A_2' \cap A_3'\]

(c) The part of \(A_2\) that overlaps neither other circle.

\[A_1' \cap A_2 \cap A_3'\]

(d) The three two-circle overlaps, without the centre.

\[(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3') \cup (A_1 \cap A_2' \cap A_3) \cup (A_1' \cap A_2 \cap A_3)\]

(e) All of \(A_3\), together with the region outside the three circles. The part of \(A_1' \cap A_2'\) that lies inside \(A_3\) is already shaded.

\[A_3 \cup (A_1' \cap A_2')\]

10Venn diagram identities

Devore §2.1, like Exercise 9, p. 57

For any events \(A\) and \(B\), use Venn diagrams to decide whether each relationship is true or false. Choose the diagram for the left side, then the diagram for the right side.

(a)

\((A \cap B')' = A' \cup B\)

Diagram for the left side, \((A \cap B')'\)

Diagram for the right side, \(A' \cup B\)

True or false?

(b)

\((A \cup B)' = A' \cup B'\)

Diagram for the left side, \((A \cup B)'\)

Diagram for the right side, \(A' \cup B'\)

True or false?

Worked solution

(a) True. \(A \cap B'\) is the part of \(A\) outside \(B\). Its complement is everything else: the overlap, the rest of \(B\), and the outside. \(A' \cup B\) shades the same three regions, so the two sides match. This is De Morgan's law applied to \(A\) and \(B'\).

(b) False. \((A \cup B)'\) is only the region outside both circles. \(A' \cup B'\) is everything except the overlap, which is a larger region. The correct identity is below.

\[(A \cup B)' = A' \cap B'\]
